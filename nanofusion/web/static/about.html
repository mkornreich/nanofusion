NanoFusion tests “guided nuclear laser fusion”. Philipp Kornreich and Carlos Hartmann of Syracuse University proposed it in a talk at Photonics North 2011. The code's author tag, pkornrei, matches. The full write-up, with sources, is in docs/what-it-simulates.md.

The idea

A very short, intense laser pulse (about 6 fs) hits a lithium-deuteride (LiD) nanocrystal and strips its electrons in under a femtosecond. The heavy nuclei stay on their lattice sites for a moment. The laser field, aligned with a crystal axis, pushes the light deuterium nuclei faster than the lithium ones, “guiding” each deuteron into the lithium nucleus ahead of it. The model puts bare deuterium (+1) and lithium-7 (+3) nuclei on LiD's lattice sites, 2.04 Å apart. It applies the laser field for about 20 fs and reports “fusion” if a pair comes within 2 fm, roughly the range of the nuclear force.

What it shows

The guiding effect is real: deuterons close in on their lithium neighbours, to 1.3 Å at 1e12 V/m and 0.56 Å at 3e12 V/m. But fusion needs 2 fm, about 28,000 times closer, and a 2.2 MeV Coulomb barrier, while the pair gains only about 5–80 eV. For the field's push to supply 2.2 MeV it would need about 10¹⁷ V/m (about 10²⁷ W/cm²). That is about 10,000 times the most intense laser ever built. At realistic strengths the model cannot produce fusion, and a “YES” at the default threshold would be a numerical artifact.

With improved physics

The “Improved physics” model has every ion interacting, a real laser pulse and measured fusion cross sections. It shows that the guiding fails: lithium-6 has the same charge-to-mass ratio as deuterium, and lithium-7 collisions reach only about 90 keV even at 10²² W/cm². Instead, the stripped crystal Coulomb-explodes in about 2 fs. A 30 nm crystal would fire deuterons at about 300 keV, giving about 0.5 fusion reactions per pulse if they hit surrounding LiD. That is detectable, but the laser delivers about 10⁷ times more energy than the fusion releases. Details are in docs/improved-model.md.

Further experiments (docs/experiments.md) found the crystal never fuses by itself. Pre-expanded crystals and longer wavelengths don't help, and colliding crystals help only a little. But a tritium catcher raises the yield about 60-fold (about 33 reactions per 30 nm crystal), and larger crystals give far more. The best fusion-to-laser energy ratio is still only about 10⁻³.

Net energy? No (docs/energy.md). Ions stopping in cold matter return at most about 0.4% of their energy as fusion, even with a perfect laser. Firing them into an already-hot D-T plasma returns 2–3 times their energy, but that needs a fusion plasma to begin with. Exploding a whole ball of crystals only breaks even as ordinary, uncompressed inertial-confinement fusion: a few mm of D-T heated to 10–30 keV with 11–78 MJ delivered at once. That is far beyond any laser, and light can't reach crystals deeper than about 10 nm.

The published routes to beat this limit don't work for these crystals either: proton–boron fuel and its “avalanche” (at most about 0.02 extra fusions per fusion), electron screening, and using the crystals as a fast-ignition spark (their explosion is isotropic and wastes over 97% of the ions). Shaped crystals don't fix this. A disk still sprays its ions in every direction. A lithium disk with a deuterium layer on one face does make a beam, but the ignitor would then need megajoules of laser in femtoseconds, tens of thousands of times the power of any laser today. The same model reproduces the Texas Petawatt cluster-fusion record, whose measured fusion-to-laser energy ratio is about 2×10⁻⁷. For comparison, the National Ignition Facility reached a target gain of 4.1 by compressing D-T fuel.

The strongest form of the original “guided head-on collisions” idea is a hollow shell whose ions are pulled onto its centre all at once (docs/implosion.md). The focus works, shrinking to under 1% of the cavity radius. But the ions' own repulsion limits how many fit into it, and deuterium and tritium arrive at different times. The best case returns about 3×10⁻⁴ of the energy the laser must supply, almost all of it from ions springing back into the cold wall.

Two routes need no hot plasma at all. Muon-catalysed fusion in solid D-T comes closest: each muon catalyses up to about 150 fusions, returning about half the energy it cost to make. Sticking to the alpha particle stops it short of breakeven, and laser-made muons are about 10⁸ times too expensive. NASA's “lattice confinement fusion” (gamma rays on erbium deuteride) returned about 4×10⁻¹⁴ of its beam energy.

More speculative ideas fare no better. A cold crystal squeezed until its nuclei tunnel together would ignite, but only at white-dwarf density (~10⁷ g/cm³, ten thousand times what laser fusion reaches). Micrometre crystals could make muons ten million times more cheaply than today's laser muon sources, yet no more cheaply than an accelerator. Spin-polarised fuel cuts breakeven energies severalfold without changing the verdict. Across every fusion regime in dense matter (screened, pycnonuclear, four published rate models), hot fuel always ignites more cheaply than cold or warm fuel at the same density.

The biggest lever found is holding the ball of crystals together (python3 tools/confined_ball.py): a thick gold tamper, a magnetic field of a few kilotesla along its wall, and heating by the fusion alphas. This is magnetised target fusion without the compression. On paper it cuts the breakeven energy about a thousandfold, from about 60 MJ to about 50 kJ in the fuel, or 10 kJ if the crystals heat the ions first. It is still not net energy. Such a target would return about 0.15 of the laser energy at breakeven, and it needs a picosecond laser 15–170 times more energetic than any that exists, a field several times the strongest laser-made ones, and an entrance hole under 1% of the wall. With the straight field that real coils make, the energy needed is about ten times higher.

Four more routes fail outright (python3 tools/further_routes.py). A fission blanket fed by crystal-made neutrons would have to be a critical reactor in all but name. D-T supports no chain reaction through its own neutrons and alphas (about 0.002 new fusions per fusion when cold). Bare ions in a magnetic trap are capped by their own charge at a few watts per cubic metre. Crystals fired as projectiles hit the same 0.4% cold-catcher limit. The model also reproduces the best nanostructure experiment so far, a nanowire-array shot that gave 1.2×10⁷ neutrons.

One new lever weakens the electrons' drag itself (python3 tools/pauli_blocking.py). In fuel squeezed until its electrons are degenerate, an ion slower than the electrons can only kick the few near the Fermi surface (Pauli blocking), so the drag stops growing with density. A crystal ion then returns its own energy at the density NIF's fuel is designed for (about 1,000 g/cm³), 250 times better than in cold matter. But paying for the squeeze leaves under 4% of the light, and fuel that dense ignites from a small hot spark anyway. The same physics lets hot proton–boron ions keep themselves hot, as Son & Fisch proposed, but only at white-dwarf density. It also frees muons stuck to fusion alphas, but a squeeze lasts about 0.1 ns, far too short for muon catalysis.

Three more walls were attacked (python3 tools/three_walls.py). Making a proton–boron ball opaque to its own X-rays helps only in white-dwarf-like matter: at NIF's design density, trapping half its X-rays takes 1.8 kg of fuel holding about 8 megatons, nearly all of it in the trapped X-rays and electron–positron pairs. The balls that do repay their heat need 40–60 times the areal density of NIF's hot spot, as published studies also find. A D-T fibre pinched by its own current needs no laser: at the 26 MA of Sandia's Z machine a sixfold squeeze, held for one Alfvén time (37 ps), repays the plasma's heat, but the field around it holds 7.5 times that heat. Channelling ions through a tritide crystal returns at most 2.3% of an aligned ion's energy (0.8% with measured values), and an exploding crystal can't aim its ions.

Every route now runs in one simulator with a common energy account (python3 -m nanofusion sim; docs/simulator.md). A search over thousands of designs, ranked by how far beyond today's technology they go, finds that with today's technology the best is muon catalysis at a tenth of breakeven. Ordinary inertial fusion, run as a yardstick, passes net electricity within ten times today's best on every demand (1.5, with a 19 MJ laser and ten times NIF's compression). A pinch followed in time, with its fuel inside a thin liner lined with frozen D-T, goes further on paper (22), but only by converging about 170 times, five times the best any implosion has managed. Combining exploding crystals with the other ideas (as a fusion spark, a muon factory, with spin-polarised fuel or blankets) never gets above about 1% of breakeven.

Untested theory is kept apart: alpha channelling and direct conversion for proton–boron, freeing stuck muons with fields, antiproton-sparked fusion, light-assisted tunnelling and spin-polarised fuel. Searched as if each worked, they mostly raise the numbers without changing the verdict: proton–boron stays a few percent of breakeven, antimatter sparks need more antiprotons than have ever been made at once, and holding the tunnelling light over the fuel costs more than the fusion it adds.

Two practical experiments close the study. For ordinary inertial fusion (docs/icf_experiment.md), a shot campaign on the National Ignition Facility changes one thing at a time. A 1-D implosion-and-burn model, which matches LLNL's own 1-D yields, puts the 2025 record (8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ of light) at 43% of a clean implosion. The campaign is mostly not novel: NIF has already shown that lowering the adiabat alone raises the yield, which the simple static model had denied. The open test is whether extra laser energy is better spent on fuel than on ablator.

For a dense D-T Z-pinch (docs/zpinch_experiment.md), a time-dependent model with the generator's circuit shows three barriers the static model missed. A cold fibre can't carry the current; a preheated one collapses radiatively and caps its own current; and stability decides the rest. On Sandia's Z, even a fully stabilised pinch reaches only 1% of breakeven; at about 70 MA a millimetre-thick fibre that stays stable for tens of Alfvén times would ignite and burn 40% of its fuel. A centimetre-wide gas column, as in Z's gas puffs, is a better load. It takes the current and heats itself, and Z could test it now with deuterium. In the model it makes net electricity at 60–80 MA, but only by converging 160–260 times and holding a few Alfvén times, on a driver being built for about 2030. Fuel inside a MagLIF-like liner, with thick frozen D-T on its wall, does better: at 80 MA it returns about four times the electricity while converging no further than MagLIF already does. The 0-D liner is only a rough guide, though: it overshoots Z's MagLIF yields tenfold and undershoots published high-current designs. The search's best liner (34 MA, a thin wall, thick ice; docs/liner_experiment.md) has never been tried. It ignites only if the fuel converges about 170 times, where no liner has managed more than about 40. A deuterium test on Z, imaging how small the fuel column gets, would settle it. MHD simulations (Athena++; tools/athena_liner.py) already predict the answer. In 2-D, a ripple of 0.1 to 1 µm on the liner breaks it up, and the fuel converges only 9 to 25 times. A first look in 3-D is no better. No one has fielded D-T in such a pinch, and Z doesn't yet handle the tritium, but preheated deuterium fibres with a tritium trace would test the model today.

Known limitations of the original model

  • No electrons, and Coulomb forces only between near neighbours (≤ 2 lattice steps). The long-range field of the whole stripped crystal is left out. That field is what gives real exploding clusters keV energies: about 14 eV × R² (R in Å) at the surface.
  • Positions and velocities are rounded to the “Precision” digits each step. At 6 digits a position between 1 and 10 nm moves in 10 fm steps, which is coarser than the 2 fm threshold.
  • The “Lorentz” term omits the field amplitude E₀·sin(ωt), so it is negligibly small. It is kept as written to match the original results.
  • Explicit Euler integration, with distances checked once per step.
